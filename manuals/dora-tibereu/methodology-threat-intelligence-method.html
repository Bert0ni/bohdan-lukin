Threat Intelligence method (TIP)

How the TI stream runs the threat intelligence phase (~4–6 weeks) as the Threat Intelligence Provider and produces a defensible TTIR document set: the TTIR + scenario matrix + evidence register.

Primary basis (DORA-applicable): ECB TIBER-EU Targeted Threat Intelligence Report Guidance (Jan 2025) §2–§3 + Annex 1 (PDF); the binding DORA TLPT RTS Annex III (full text); and the practical Norges Bank TIBER-NO TTIR Guidance v1.2. The deliverable's required content is defined in the TTIR template. Recognised CTI tradecraft (intelligence cycle, Diamond Model, source grading, CREST CTI / CBEST STAR) is used in support of — never instead of — these requirements, and is marked (tradecraft).

The output is not a raw OSINT dump. It is contextualised reasoning about which attacks are realistic for this entity: target intelligence (the entity's reality) and threat intelligence (who would attack and how) alternate and supplement each other, and must never be analysed in silos.

A. The TIP engagement — operational sub-phases#

The TI phase runs as a mini intelligence cycle (tradecraft: direction → collection → processing → analysis → dissemination), instantiated as six working steps inside the ~4–6 week window:

A1. Direction — kickoff, questionnaire & intelligence requirements#

  • Inputs: the validated SSD (CIFs, key systems, flags, objectives) and the launch alignment. The CT and TM have already agreed scope before the TIP starts drafting.
  • Business-overview session + questionnaire. The entity provides a strategic understanding of itself at the start of the phase (ECB Annex 1 "business overview input"). Run this as a structured intake — see the TI scoping questionnaire. Much of it is obtainable from open source, but having the entity supply it lets the TIP spend its effort on analysis and digital-footprint work, not on basic facts. Treat anything commercially sensitive pragmatically.
  • Set intelligence requirements (tradecraft: PIRs + a collection plan). Turn the SSD CIFs/flags into explicit questions the collection must answer (see §B "intelligence input").
  • Agree OPSEC & deconfliction rules (see §D): the TI phase must not alert the blue team.

A2. Collection — target + threat intelligence#

Run the two streams in parallel (detail in §B). Target: digital footprint on a per-CIF basis (people, processes, technologies). Threat: GTL + actor research. Passive by default; any active reconnaissance is tightly gated (§C).

A3. Processing & analysis — contextualise, then prioritise actors#

  • Contextualise the CIFs — enrich the SSD CIF list (CIF → sub-functions → justification) with what GTL, business overview, footprint and intel input reveal about why this entity is a target.
  • Contextualise the flags — translate each SSD flag into how a real actor would reach it (flag-setting stays fluid; it can still change in the RTTP and during active testing).
  • Threat-actor longlist → shortlist — categorise actors, then name specific actors; score and rank by capability × intent (× relevance); document the scoring method and why rejected actors were dropped (see §F).

A4. Scenario development — longlist → shortlist#

Build a longlist (expect ≥6; Norges: ~6–10) of high-level scenarios covering every CIF in scope, then select ≥3 for the shortlist (see §G). The longlist must be rich enough to drive a substantiated CT/TM debate.

A5. TTIR drafting, scenario-selection meeting & flag re-validation#

  • Draft per the TTIR template (ECB §2 / RTS Annex III spine).
  • Scenario-selection meeting (CT, TM, TIP, RT): the RTTM assesses feasibility, timing, TTP match, flag reachability without unacceptable impact, and leg-ups/dependencies. The CTL selects the ≥3 shortlist scenarios on TIP recommendation + TM input.
  • Re-validate the SSD flags with the entity on the basis of the selected scenarios.
  • TTIR meeting; CT forwards to TM; the CT and TM must agree/attest to the TTIR before the test can progress (TIBER-NO).

A6. Handover to Red Team & standing support#

Deliver TTIR + selected scenarios + evidence to the RTTM and log it in the TI-to-RT handover register. The TIP stays available to supply additional intelligence during active testing. Maintain TI/RT separation throughout (single-provider model). After the test, share the TTIR with the entity's internal CTI team so they can validate their own intelligence and reduce the targetable footprint discovered.

B. What we collect — the two streams#

Target intelligence (the entity's digital footprint — per CIF)#

Attack surface of people, processes and technologies, both intentionally published and unintentionally leaked:

  • Attack-surface mapping; Certificate Transparency (crt.sh) mining; multi-source subdomain enumeration; technology fingerprinting.
  • Shadow-IT discovery; exposed cloud storage / management interfaces; exposed services.
  • Look-alike / typo-squat domains that could impersonate the entity.
  • Exposed/leaked credentials — employee usernames & passwords, breach & infostealer telemetry, paste sites, dark-web listings.
  • People footprint — employees' social-media info, job roles & assumed privileged access, contact details useful for social engineering.
  • Third-party / supply-chain relationships and their security posture.
  • Where relevant, physical targeting information (ways to access premises).

Threat intelligence (who would attack, and how)#

  • Generic Threat Landscape (GTL): if the jurisdiction (e.g. a national/Nordic GTL) publishes one, use it as the foundational basis; otherwise the TIP develops a general landscape view in the TTIR.
  • Environment analysis: geopolitical, economic, and technological/sector trends.
  • Relevant actors: financially-motivated OCGs, APT/state-nexus (incl. Russia-nexus), hacktivists, insiders, supply-chain — with motivation, capability, campaign history.
  • TTPs mapped to the latest MITRE ATT&CK.

Intelligence input — the questions collection must answer (ECB §3.2.4)#

  • Target environment: what would help an actor understand the CIF-supporting network? What do network diagrams/exposed info reveal about where CIFs sit and how they interconnect? What vulnerabilities in CIF-supporting systems are exposed? Which infra has been targeted before? What are the most prevalent/critical sector vulnerabilities likely present here?
  • Social engineering: which personnel (by role / privileged access) could be targeted? What contact details & credentials of key people are discoverable?
  • Third parties / MSPs: how do they affect the entity's posture? Which have been or could be compromised, and how would that give an actor a route in?

Tradecraft lenses: strategic ("who & why"), operational ("when/where/how", campaign trend analysis), tactical ("how & what", tool/TTP-level) intelligence; the Diamond Model (adversary– capability–infrastructure–victim) and F3EAD for structuring collection and analysis.

C. Passive vs active collection (critical TIBER rule)#

Passive (default)Active reconnaissance
WhatNo interaction that touches the target's live systems/people in a way the defender could notice: OSINT, CT logs, Shodan/Censys historical data, breach dumps, dark web, public records, HUMINT (overt/covert, ethical)Direct interaction that could be observed: scanning, probing, crafted contact, enumeration against the entity's own assets
Who / whenTIP, throughout the TI phaseOnly the RTT, in the TI phase, at the TIP's request and after CT + TM approval
Hard rule—Must not alert the blue team or reveal that a TIBER test is underway

The TI phase must not tip off the Blue Team. The BT is unaware a test is happening; any active step that risks disclosure is prohibited unless explicitly gated through the RTT + CT + TM. When in doubt, stay passive and record the gap as an intelligence limitation.

D. Ethics, legality & OPSEC#

  • OSINT = overt, publicly available. HUMINT = overt or covert from human sources — always with strong, documented ethical conduct and within legal bounds; no entrapment, no unlawful access.
  • Handle leaked credentials/PII lawfully; never authenticate with discovered credentials during TI.
  • OPSEC: attributable infrastructure, codename discipline, need-to-know, encrypted comms/data rooms per the project charter.
  • Privacy: keep real personal data out of this repository; in a live engagement follow the agreed data-handling rules.

E. Source grading & confidence (tradecraft)#

Record, per source, its reliability and the information's credibility (Admiralty/NATO grading A–F / 1–6) and give each analytic judgement a confidence level (high/medium/low) with reasoning. Keep it all in the evidence register so the selection is traceable, defensible and repeatable — every claim ties back to a source reference.

F. Threat-actor assessment & profiling#

  1. Categorise actor classes and assess how each relates to this entity.
  2. Name specific actors deemed most relevant, with evidence-based reasoning on motivation/intent and why they would specifically target these CIFs/flags (narrative, not a label).
  3. Map actors → CIFs with the motivation per cell (see the ECB §3.3.3 matrix pattern; use the scenario matrix).
  4. Profile the shortlisted actors on (Norges §3.4):
    • Motivation (financial / geopolitical / …), Goals/intent (concrete objective),
    • Sophistication (script-kiddie → nation-state), Agility (inflexible → adaptable),
    • Perseverance (opportunistic → motivated), Purpose (meandering → direct).
  5. Threat level per actor/scenario from capability × intent (e.g. "Capability: Very high, Intent: High → Threat: High"); take the highest threat-severity profiles into scenarios.
  6. Explain exclusions — why other actor classes were dropped.

G. Scenario development#

  • Longlist (≥6; Norges ~6–10): high-level, each built on the reconnaissance, targeting a specific CIF with the actor's specific intent and likely TTPs. Scenarios target every CIF in scope.
  • Shortlist (≥3), selected by the CTL on: TIP recommendation & threat-led nature · TM input · RTT feasibility judgement · the entity's size/complexity/risk profile · breadth & depth across CIFs · (multi-party) at least one scenario covering the ICT third-party provider's systems.
  • Coverage (mandatory): at least one scenario each for compromised availability, integrity, and confidentiality (each "includes but is not limited to" that aspect); address authenticity where the flag does (per RTS/SSD).
  • Scenario X: at most one of the selected may be non-threat-led / forward-looking (hybrid, novel TTPs, AI/quantum/deepfake, "out of the box"), customised to the entity. If none is set in the TI phase, one scenario may be converted to Scenario X during active testing with CT+TM+RTT agreement.
  • Backward vs forward-looking: don't rely on historical data only — forecast where the actor goes next, but keep a clear distinction between evidence-based (backward) and hypothetical (forward) TTPs. Consider sophistication, agility, directedness, and sector knowledge.
  • Other selection factors: noise created (may re-order execution), differing motivations, and the entity's stated learning goals.

Scenario anatomy (per shortlisted scenario)#

  • Name — actor + goal, e.g. "SC-A: [OCG] targets [Entity] payment service for financial gain".
  • Threat level — capability / intent / overall threat.
  • End-to-end attack path across prepare → infiltrate → entrench → execute (the RT's in → through → out), from no access to full CIF compromise and flag achievement.
  • Mapping — threat actor → objective/flag → ATT&CK tactic → technique → procedure(s).
  • Links — CIF, flag, impact dimension (C/I/A/authenticity), evidence refs, intelligence confidence.

Shortlisted scenarios are then worked out in full detail (narrative + detailed attack path + MITRE refs); a selected Scenario X keeps some flexibility for the RT phase.

H. TTIR structure & report examples#

The TTIR has no mandated format — any format is fine provided all §2 / RTS Annex III content is present. Two reference structures:

ECB §2 / RTS Annex III content spine (the compliance backbone — see TTIR template Part A): scope of research · digital-footprint assessment · threat-landscape analysis (geopolitical/economic/technological) · threat profiles · ≥3 end-to-end scenarios (A/I/C) · optional Scenario X.

Norges TIBER-NO "example TTIR structure" (practical skeleton):

  1. Executive summary
  2. Business overview from an intelligence perspective
  3. Intelligence on the entity's digital presence (digital footprint)
  4. Threat actors — threat-actor assessment → threat-actor profiling
  5. High-level threat scenarios — per scenario: threat level (actor + scenario) + attack flow with TTPs
  6. Appendices — TTP tables, OSINT raw data, scoring methodology

Our house approach keeps the full ECB-aligned layout in the TTIR template with the concise TIBER-NO layout available for smaller engagements, and splits structured data into the scenario matrix and evidence register so the narrative stays readable.

I. Passive OSINT tooling (indicative — keep current & licensed)#

Attack surface / DNS: crt.sh & CT logs, Amass/subfinder, Shodan, Censys, FOFA. People/footprint: LinkedIn & social, hunter.io-style email patterns, Maltego, SpiderFoot. Breach/credential: HIBP, DeHashed, IntelX, infostealer-log feeds. Dark web & closed sources: vetted CTI feeds, marketplace monitoring. Always within the ethics/legal/OPSEC rules in §D; prefer passive data and record any active step as gated per §C.
